CHAPTER 09

Reconstruct state approximately

Chapter 1 rebuilt a three-entry window by replaying three inputs. Chapter 4 then encountered state that DeepInfer deliberately leaves out of its persistent cache. Rebuilding that state sounds like the same operation. The difficulty is what an entry contains: a later layer caches representations produced by earlier layers, and those representations may depend on positions outside the window. DeepInfer shortens prefill by accepting an approximation in this local state, while preserving the full source for another part of the cache.

The question

If each layer reads only three positions, can replaying the last three inputs rebuild two layers’ caches exactly? What changes if both layers take their cached entries from the same shared input?

The lab starts with the integers 1 to 8, two layers and a window of three. Each layer adds the mean of its visible entries to its current input. One version caches each layer’s own input; the other always caches the shared original input. It compares a full prefill with a replay of inputs 6, 7 and 8, then uses each resulting cache to process the next input, 9:

Shared inputs preserve both results. Per-layer inputs change the last prefill result from 28 to 57∕2, and the next decode result from 32 to 193∕6. The error survives the handover from prefill to decode. We will find the same dependency distinction in DeepInfer’s full block-KV projections and truncated sliding-window attention (SWA).

9.1 Where exact window recovery stops

The lab uses identity KV projections: the cached row is the input row itself, and the attention weights are uniform. For a layer input hi and cached values vj, its rule is

hi′ = h i + 1 |Ji|∑ j∈Jivj,Ji = {max ⁡ (s,i − W + 1),… ⁡ ,i}.

Here W is the window and s is the first available position. The full calculation starts at zero; suffix replay starts later. The addition of hi is a residual: it carries that row’s input into its output. This deliberately small rule isolates the dependencies without introducing softmax or floating-point round-off.

The two versions differ in one line of the layer loop. boundary is the unchanged input, while hidden is the preceding layer’s output:

Consider the per-layer version. The first layer’s full output at the position holding 6 is 6 + (4 + 5 + 6)∕3 = 11. Replaying from 6 loses 4 and 5, giving 6 + 6 = 12. At the positions holding 7 and 8, the replay gives 27∕2 and 15, instead of 13 and 15. These three outputs become the second layer’s KV rows. Its last query therefore reads different values even though all three positions are present:

full: 15 + 11 + 13 + 15 3 = 28,replay: 15 + 12 + 27∕2 + 15 3 = 57 2 .

The first changed cache entry is in layer 2, at zero-based position 5. Recovering the raw entries recovered layer 1’s KV, but did not recover the representations needed by layer 2.

In the shared version, both layers read the original values 6, 7 and 8 for the last position. That position’s residual is also unchanged: it had its whole window in the preceding layer. Its final result is 8 + 7 + 7 = 22. The retained shared KV is exact even though some earlier retained hidden rows are not. More generally, with shared KV, row-local operations and a replay that contains the last row’s window, the last row stays exact by induction over layers: its own previous representation and its visible shared KV are unchanged. This reasoning also permits queries that depend on that row’s previous representation. It does not permit cached KV that depends on other changed hidden rows.

9.1.1 A window grows backwards across layers

To recover three KV inputs at layer 2, first recover the three layer-1 outputs that produce them. Their combined input span contains five positions. Replaying inputs 4 through 8 therefore restores the retained KV and final output of our two-layer model exactly. The lab checks this equality with fractions.

For a window of W entries at layer D, each preceding layer adds W − 1 positions to the left. The current position is already included, so it must not be counted twice. The span is

R = W + (D − 1)(W − 1). (9.1)

For 20 layers with a 128-position window, R = 2,541. The deployment Q&A describes the conservative product 20 × 128 = 2,560 [2]. Both express the growth of SWA dependencies across layers; 2,541 is the tighter span under the inclusive causal-window convention.

This counts input positions required by the local dependency chain, conditional on shared inputs and global KV already being available. It is not the sum of all layer-row computations. Those intervals can shrink as the computation moves upwards. Nor does it account for the work of producing the shared input. The lab checks these claims for windows 2 to 4 and one to three layers, on every 0/1 input two positions longer than the span: 5,456 cases. A shared-input replay of one window and a per-layer replay of R positions each reproduce the final row, the retained KV and one subsequent decode step. For every window and depth, replaying R − 1 positions changes the final row or the decode result for at least one input, so no shorter replay is exact for this rule. These are exact statements about the teaching rule, not about DeepInfer’s kernels.

9.2 Where DeepInfer puts the boundary

DeepInfer’s V4.1 path combines two sources of KV. Consumer layers project block KV from a shared representation produced at a boundary in the model. Their SWA KV instead comes from each layer’s own input. Thus the engine can preserve the source of block KV while shortening the consumer computation that builds local SWA state.

The design draws on YOCO (You Only Cache Once). The terminology comes from three places:

CED means Causal Encoder-Decoder. The model report explicitly credits YOCO as an inspiration. The YOCO paper’s cross-decoder reads a shared global KV cache and permits prefill to exit early without changing the final output. CED uses layer-dependent global-KV projections from shared hidden states and retains a separate per-layer SWA path. That local path requires reconstruction when decoder prefill is shortened [68, 69]. The table maps roles; it does not make these architectures identical.

Our concrete case is a long prompt with no prefix-cache hit, processed in one chunk. We explicitly enable compact mode. The producer layers process all prompt rows. At the boundary, two row layouts emerge (Figure 9.1). We first follow those layouts, then examine how DeepInfer selects the retained positions.

Both operations cover source positions excluded from consumer attention and MLP computation. Layers that reuse another layer’s block KV need not repeat that projection. In our no-hit case, truncation has not removed the inputs required to construct block KV. This is exactness of the dependency, not a claim that every kernel layout yields bitwise-identical arithmetic [22].

There is a second kind of cross-layer sharing in the repository. SharedLayerTopology maps layers to sources for KV, index keys, selected indices and candidate blocks. These segmented maps are distinct from sharing the boundary representation. Its validation requires a KV source at the YOCO boundary: a share-KV segment cannot cross that boundary (cross_layer.py, lines 114–116). These maps govern reuse of computed entries between layers; the shared hidden path determines the input from which those entries are projected [72].

With the flag false, split separates query and local KV components produced from hidden_states. With it true, a separate projection takes shared_hidden_bf16. These correspond to the two dependency choices in the opening experiment. In the inspected V4.1 configuration, a consumer row near the retained region’s left edge loses SWA neighbours. Its changed output can become a later consumer’s KV input. Preserving full block KV does not repair that local dependency.

The toy’s shared-input case explains why moving KV to a shared source can remove this particular dependency. It does not establish that enabling the flag would make DeepInfer’s whole computation exact: the rest of the model, indexing and numerical kernels would need their own checks. The engine we are reading uses the per-layer choice.

9.3 Which rows can a consumer see?

Let the prompt length be L, the retained count K, and the window W. The retained region starts at skeep = L − K, clipped by the actual prompt extent. For our small diagram, L = 12, K = 4, W = 3. Full-source block KV still covers all 12 positions; consumer queries retain positions 8 through 11 (Figure 9.2).

rect_lo is the retained-region start; valid_start is the earliest valid state position. For a query at position i, the lower bound is

ℓi = clamp ⁡ (max ⁡ (i − W + 1,skeep,svalid),0,i + 1).

Visible positions run from ℓi through i. If ℓi = i + 1, that interval is empty. The comment says that the valid-state bound prevents attention from reading zeroed slots (author’s translation, lines 151–154) [38].

For query 8 in the diagram, the usual window is 6–8 but the kept-region bound raises the start to 8. Query 10 sees 8–10, a full three-position window. Its current layer has all three positions, yet their representations may already carry earlier truncation error. This is the distinction the lab exposed. The Q&A’s max ⁡ (s,i − 127) expresses the replay case with a 128-position window; the code also carries the valid-state bound and the empty-interval case.

9.3.3 Modes, defaults and limits

off keeps the ordinary prefill. mask retains all query rows but narrows consumer SWA visibility. compact uses that narrowed visibility and removes query rows, apart from placeholders. Mask therefore supplies a comparison for the compact layout. It removes no consumer rows; only its count of visible SWA pairs falls. The benchmark’s help text says that mask computes every consumer token and saves no work [15]. Both enabled modes truncate the local dependency.

Later, state_size_to_disk is assigned only the stored SWA region’s inference-layout byte count. Consumer SWA remains in the dropped region alongside compression state. The transfer from the prefill engine to the decode engine (PE to CE in the docstring and Q&A) includes that region, because decode needs it. “Dropped” thus means excluded from prefix persistence, not released at the end of prefill [23].

This policy makes the next prefill rebuild consumer SWA instead of retrieving it. With truncation enabled, that reconstruction is approximate. With it off, consumer computation is full only when nothing is restored. load_state_from_store_to_prefill_cache copies stored state into a prefill slot after a hit. Its first statement asserts that truncation is not off whenever the model has dropped SWA layers (lines 836–837). A hit without truncation would need consumer SWA for positions before the hit, and nothing stored supplies it. The release therefore ties restored prefix state to truncated consumer replay, although the cache partition itself is the same in either mode [23].

The Q&A identifies the first 20 layers as the stored V4.1 layers, and the model report gives 20 encoder and 20 decoder layers. These counts are documented model settings, not numbers implied by the generic predicate [2, 69].

9.4.1 The other bounded replay

The deployment Q&A uses bounded replay for two operations. Our source path so far is the decoder operation:

For encoder recovery, the Q&A describes reusing the replayed prefix’s block KV without recomputing or overwriting it. Only its SWA is rebuilt; the uncached suffix produces both kinds of KV. The production cache manager chooses a block-aligned hit and backs off one block when qualifying SWA state is missing. That cache-management implementation is outside the release [2].

The released hooks support parts of such a path, but do not drive it. swa_valid_start is initialised or reset to zero in KvCacheState; it has a binding, but the inspected Python code contains no writer. The scheduler reserves and clears zero_states without filling it. It stores truncate_len without reading it. Following those names alone would therefore not reconstruct the production hit-recovery algorithm [6, 8].

The documented hit cap also leaves an integration question. Capping a hit at L − 134 guarantees at least 134 uncached rows. Those rows could supply the shared hidden inputs needed by decoder replay; that connection is an inference about the unreleased driver. An aligned hit exactly at the cap leaves 134 rows, not the README’s 140. The released metadata code makes the gap concrete. With qstart = L − 134, skeep = L − 140 and W = 128, the first query’s lower bound is L − 140 unless svalid is raised. Its visibility therefore includes six consumer ring entries from before the hit (start_slot is 122). Restoring a hit writes only the stored region, so nothing in the release fills those entries. This is our reading of the metadata code; we have not executed it. Block alignment and an SWA miss can lengthen the suffix, but neither supplies a universal answer for the exact-cap case. The release does not establish how that service policy and the suggested keep setting are combined. Chapter 11 will discuss the documented policy with this limit intact.

At length 64 there is nothing to remove. At 1,024, the ratio is about 0.5684; at 16,384 it is about 0.5043. This explains the scale of the Q&A’s “nearly halving” claim, rather than reproducing a model-specific FLOP count.

DeepInfer’s actual estimator retains work that the row proxy omits. calc_kproj_flops takes the full query range for block-KV projections. calc_attn_flops combines full and consumer row terms, SWA pair terms, and global-attention pair terms. The boundary normalisation-related contractions also retain full-row work. calc_non_attn_flops weights producer and consumer rows using their own coefficients. Chapter 2’s FP8-equivalent accounting still applies; these counts do not turn directly into execution time [14].

The repository’s tests/engine/test_flops_estimator.py compares the native estimator with per-layer, per-query accounting, including off, mask, compact and chunk cases. It requires model metadata and the compiled extension. Our lab checks the translated range and pair formulas, not that native test or the full model’s cost [74].

9.5.3 Equivalence needs the right comparison

The opening experiment compares shortened computation with full computation. The released YOCO checks ask a different question: does compact match mask? Their L1 check compares metadata, visible keys and slots. L2 compares model log probabilities and top-token results along a fixed teacher-forced trajectory. Both modes use truncated SWA, so agreement between them cannot establish that truncation preserves the full model [75].

The script also supports an untruncated baseline by setting keep_tokens=0; it then selects off. The capability to produce a baseline is not a published error measurement. No full-versus-truncated error measurement was found in the inspected release. The model report states that the reconstructed decoder SWA KV is not mathematically equivalent to a full decoder pass. It reports a negligible effect on response quality, and simulates the same replay during post-training [69]. These are the authors’ findings, not an error bound reproduced here. The last point means the approximation is partly a model property: post-training adapted the model to it, so it is not only an engine shortcut.

An evaluation of the approximation would hold inputs and the continuation trajectory fixed, then compare full and truncated intermediate state and log probabilities. A separate task evaluation would ask whether those differences matter to answers. Exact state equality, numerical agreement, and acceptable model quality are three different claims. Chapter 10 returns to the contracts checked by the released equivalence tests.

9.6 Experiments to try next

  1. Use equation (9.1) for four layers and a five-position window. List the intervals required at each layer to recover the final window. Which number counts input positions, and which counts layer-row computations?
  2. In lab 19, retain four inputs instead of three or five. Find the first differing second-layer cache entry. Is the final prefill result exact? Check one subsequent decode step as well.
  3. A prompt has 12 positions, keep count four and window three. For a chunk covering positions 4–7, find the compact query, its SWA visibility and its write slot. Explain why a query can execute without updating the ring.
  4. Switch truncation from compact to off. Does _is_dropped_swa_layer move consumer SWA into the stored region? Trace where that state is still needed before it can be released. Then follow a prefix hit into load_state_from_store_to_prefill_cache. Why must it refuse to run?
  5. A block-aligned hit leaves 134 uncached rows. Which part of a 140-row retained suffix lies before the hit? Identify the released evidence and the missing service behaviour needed to say how those rows are supplied.
  6. Mask and compact produce identical log probabilities. State what this establishes and propose a different comparison for the approximation error.
